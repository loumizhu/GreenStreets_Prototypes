The 17 questions behind this screen

Industry best practice, phrased as the questions a designer should be able to answer before drawing a dashboard — each one with the answer this page commits to. Reviewer aid, not product UI.

A · Purpose — who, what decision, how fast
1Who is the one person this screen is for, and what is their actual job?
A retailer compliance admin. Their job is not analysis, it is chasing evidence before a filing date. Multi-audience dashboards fail — the finance view and the supplier view are separate screens, not extra widgets here.
2What decision does it drive, and how often is that decision made?
“Who do I chase today so the return files on time?” — daily. Daily cadence means an operational dashboard: ranked work and live status, not free-form exploration.
3What must a reader understand in the first five seconds?
Three things: not ready to file · 182 items sit with you · 12 days left. The verdict block exists only to say that; everything below it is the explanation.
B · Every number — is it good, is it moving, is it real
4For each metric: is that good? Compared with what?
No bare numbers. Every tile carries a target, a target date, the remaining gap and a progress track — “48/64 · 75% · target 64 by 30 Sep · 16 to go”.
5Which way is it moving, and fast enough to make the date?
A week-on-week delta and an 8-week sparkline on every tile — and the chart converts the rate into a date: 61 approvals a week clears the queue on 18 Sep, nine days after the deadline.
6Would anyone behave differently if this number changed? If not, cut it.
The old three-series “in progress” bars changed no decision. Replaced with submitted vs approved, which is the one comparison that reveals the bottleneck is internal.
7Is every number on the same denominator — and does it agree with the page it drills into?
All product figures are /64 (the catalogue's real total) and supplier figures /380. The previous version mixed “/20” tiles with a “380 products” footer over a 64-product catalogue.
C · Structure — where the eye lands
8Does the single most important thing occupy the top-left?
Readers scan F/Z and spend most of their attention on the top and the left. The verdict runs across the top, the four comparable KPIs sit second, the tables sit last.
9Can the decision be made without scrolling?
Verdict + KPI row + the top of the queue fit one viewport — the decision layer. Trend, funnel and tables are deliberately below the fold as the explain layer.
10Is each display medium the right one — and is any variety meaningful?
One bar chart (two series over time), four identical sparklines, one funnel. No second chart type doing the same job, and no 38px donut encoding three shares.
D · Action — can they do anything about it
11Can the reader act here, or only look?
Every queue row, deadline and document carries its action inline — chase, remind, request, open the queue. Nothing is a dead end and nothing needs a second screen to start.
12Does each item say why it matters, in plain language?
Each row states the consequence and the date it bites — “blocks 41 of 64 Declarations”, “becomes a blocker on 14 Oct” — not just the fault code.
13What does this screen look like on a good day?
The empty state is designed, not left to chance. Filter the queue to a category with nothing outstanding to see it.
E · Trust — would you bet a filing on it
14How old is this data, and what scope am I looking at?
A freshness stamp and explicit scope chips (period, markets, counts in scope) sit above the verdict. An undated dashboard cannot be acted on.
15Does it survive being read with no colour?
Every status is a glyph + a word (✓ Conformant · ! At risk · ✕ Non-conformant). The old coloured dots carried the meaning in hue alone — WCAG 1.4.1.
16Does it survive small text, a tablet, and a screen reader?
Nothing below 11px, secondary text on the 74% ink not the 62%, breakpoints at 1180/900/680 with tables that scroll and 40px touch targets, a real h1/h2 outline, and reduced-motion honoured.
17Can the reader take the data away?
“Export view (CSV)” exports what is on screen, filters and all. Data etiquette: never trap the numbers in the picture.
